Each layer of the AI supply chain buys an input and sells something more useful. The supplier's revenue becomes the next company's cost, yet each can earn a margin by changing what that input can do.
</Lede>

Nvidia captures the first margin by selling hardware above its production cost. Infrastructure providers spread that purchase across years of compute sales. Their economics depend on keeping the equipment busy long enough to recover the investment.

Model companies buy that compute and sell intelligence. The chart's 85% serving-only margin falls to 52% after all compute: delivering another answer can be cheap while developing the capability remains expensive. These percentages use different cost boundaries from the hardware and infrastructure figures.

Customers turn intelligence into completed work. The assumed 2–10× return depends on how much value survives integration, review and mistakes. It is a benefit estimate, not a measured customer margin.

The interesting shift happens when an input gets cheaper. Lower compute costs can expand model margins. Lower model prices can expand application margins. Competition can pass either saving further downstream to customers.

Margin therefore depends on who can keep the value created at each transformation. For an application founder, that means owning a useful result people will pay for, while making it progressively cheaper to deliver.
