## The Law
There is a pattern in human nature as old as power itself: those who fear their own inadequacy do not seek to overcome it — they seek to protect themselves from its exposure.
They build walls where they should build competence. They negotiate immunity where they should earn trust. They lock doors that should remain open.
In kingdoms, this creates tyrants who cannot be advised. In corporations, it creates executives who cannot be challenged. In startups, it creates founders who cannot be removed — and companies that cannot survive.
The protection a man builds around his weakness becomes the ceiling that limits everyone around him. This is the law, and it does not forgive.


## The Story
Picture this: three founders sit down to structure a company. One brings technology. One brings operations. One brings distribution — a community of almost 300k developers built around his previous business, the kind of audience most startups would kill for.
Then the founder with distribution makes a condition: he needs protection. A guarantee that he can't be fired from the CEO position.
It sounded reasonable at the time. He was contributing a real asset, and he wanted security in return. A fair trade. Smart safeguarding.
So we rationalized it. We agreed. We signed. We moved on.
We didn't see it for what it was — a compromise with consequences we couldn't yet imagine. We had just installed the first ceiling, and we wouldn't recognize it until we hit it.


## The Paradigm: Defensive Identity

Some people anchor their security in position rather than competence, not because they are malicious but because they are afraid — afraid of being exposed, afraid of real comparison, afraid of being replaced.
Instead of earning trust through results, they lock in protection through structure. And the dangerous part is that this is invisible in everyday conversation. Social skills mask it. Confidence covers it. You won't hear it in what they say — you'll only see it in what they refuse to risk.
The demand for a protection clause wasn't negotiation. It was confession. It said: I don't trust myself to earn this seat through performance, so I need to guarantee it through contract.
We didn't hear the confession. We heard the reasonable argument.


## The Lesson
A protection clause is not inherently unreasonable. When someone brings a real asset to the table — an audience, a network, capital, intellectual property — wanting security in return is rational negotiation. But the existence of the clause is already a marker.

It reveals how someone thinks about risk, accountability, and their own position. It tells you that when pressure comes, their instinct is to protect rather than adapt. It tells you that somewhere underneath the confident exterior, there is a fear of being judged on results alone.
This doesn't make them a bad person. It makes them a dangerous cofounder.

Because startups don't reward protection. They reward adaptation. And when the company needs to evolve — when the market shifts, when the product fails, when the strategy must change — the founder who built walls around themselves becomes the wall everyone else hits.

If your cofounder needs a clause that guarantees they can't be removed, don't dismiss it as a red flag and don't accept it as mere negotiation. Understand it for what it is: a confession of how they'll behave when removal is the only thing that could save the company.
The clause may be justified. The pattern it reveals is still the pattern.


## BOTTOM LINE

The walls men build to protect themselves become the prisons that contain them, so the founder who couldn't be fired was never free.
And my story was also trapped with him.

Before you sign anything with a cofounder, ask yourself: are they optimizing for the startup's ability to win, or for their own ability to stay? The answer will determine your ceiling.
